A Glossary of Victorian Furniture Terms Every Collector Should Know

Antique listings, auction catalogs, and dealer descriptions for Victorian furniture are full of vocabulary that simply doesn't come up anywhere else — words like "escutcheon," "ormolu," and "what-not" that can leave even a generally well-read shopper reaching for a dictionary. Knowing this vocabulary isn't just about sounding knowledgeable; it genuinely helps you read listings accurately, ask better questions, and understand exactly what a dealer or appraiser is describing.

This glossary organizes the most useful Victorian furniture terms into a few practical categories: furniture forms, construction and joinery, style and ornament, materials and finishes, and hardware. Keep it bookmarked as a quick reference the next time a listing description leaves you puzzled.

Furniture Forms

These are the specific types of pieces you'll most often encounter in Victorian furniture listings and parlors, each designed for a particular function that may no longer be obvious from the name alone.

Balloon-back chair — A side or dining chair with a curved, rounded backrest resembling the outline of a balloon, extremely common throughout the Victorian period.

Canterbury — A small stand with divided compartments, originally designed to hold sheet music or magazines, often featuring turned legs and a carrying handle.

Chaise longue (fainting couch) — An upholstered reclining seat with one raised end, popular in Victorian parlors for both genuine rest and decorative effect.

Commode — In furniture terms, a chest of drawers or cabinet, sometimes housing a chamber pot in earlier or more utilitarian examples, not to be confused with the modern use of the word.

Credenza — A sideboard or cabinet, typically used for dining room storage and display, often featuring a combination of doors and drawers.

Davenport desk — A small, compact writing desk with a hinged, sloped writing surface and a set of small drawers, popular for letter-writing in Victorian parlors.

Étagère (what-not) — A tiered, open shelving unit designed for displaying curios, books, and decorative collectibles; "what-not" is the more colloquial English-language term for essentially the same form.

Hall tree — A combination coat rack, umbrella stand, and often a mirror, designed for entryways.

Pier table / pier mirror — A table or mirror designed to be placed against the wall section ("pier") between two windows, often paired together as a matching set.

Secretary desk — A writing desk combining a drop-front or fall-front writing surface with drawers below and often a bookcase or cabinet above.

Settee — A small sofa, typically seating two to three people, common in Victorian parlors.

Sideboard — A long, low case piece used in dining rooms for storage and serving, typically featuring a combination of cabinets and drawers.

Slipper chair — A low, armless upholstered chair, originally designed to make it easier to put on slippers or shoes, later adopted more broadly as an accent seating form.

Vitrine — A glass-fronted display cabinet, used for showcasing china, curios, or other decorative collections.

Washstand — A case piece, often topped with marble for water resistance, designed to hold a basin and pitcher for washing before the widespread adoption of indoor plumbing.

Wooton desk — A distinctive, highly compartmentalized patent desk produced by the Wooton Desk Company, prized today for its elaborate fold-out storage system.

Construction and Joinery

These terms describe how a piece was actually built, and they're often more useful for authentication and dating than style or appearance alone, since construction methods changed in fairly traceable ways as the century progressed.

Case piece — General term for any furniture item built around a box-like frame, such as a dresser, cabinet, or bookcase, as distinct from seating or table forms.

Dovetail joint — An interlocking joint, typically used to join drawer sides to fronts, with trapezoid-shaped "fingers" that lock together; hand-cut dovetails are irregular, while machine-cut dovetails (common from the 1870s onward) are uniform.

Dust panel — A thin horizontal panel installed between drawers inside a case piece, intended to keep dust from one drawer falling into the one below.

Hide glue — A natural, water-soluble adhesive widely used in 19th-century furniture construction, including for attaching veneer; its reversibility (it can be softened with heat and moisture) makes it useful for traditional repairs.

Inlay — Decorative material, often contrasting wood, shell, or metal, set into a recessed pattern on a wood surface, flush with the surrounding surface.

Laminated wood — Thin layers of wood glued together with grain running in alternating directions, then often steamed and pressed into curved shapes; closely associated with mid-Victorian Rococo Revival rosewood furniture.

Marquetry — A more elaborate, pictorial form of inlay using multiple wood veneers (and sometimes other materials) to create detailed decorative scenes or patterns.

Mortise and tenon — A traditional joinery method where a projecting tenon on one piece fits into a corresponding mortise (slot) on another, used widely in chair and table frame construction.

Secondary wood — Less expensive wood used for structural elements and hidden surfaces (drawer sides, case backs) not meant to be visible, as distinct from the "show wood" used for visible surfaces.

Veneer — A thin sheet of decorative wood applied over a less expensive or less attractive base wood, allowing expensive or highly figured woods to be used economically on visible surfaces.

Style and Ornament

These terms identify the specific revival movements and decorative motifs that give each Victorian sub-style its recognizable look.

Aesthetic Movement — A late Victorian design movement emphasizing "art for art's sake," often incorporating Japanese-inspired (Anglo-Japanese) motifs like bamboo turning, asymmetry, and stylized cranes or fans.

Cartouche — An ornamental, often oval or shield-shaped decorative element, frequently carved into case pieces and seating frames as a centerpiece motif.

Eastlake style — A late Victorian style favoring rectilinear forms and shallow incised geometric carving, named for (though not strictly designed by) design writer Charles Eastlake.

Egg-and-dart molding — A classical decorative molding pattern alternating egg-shaped and dart- or arrow-shaped elements, used as an architectural-style accent on furniture.

Gothic Revival — An early Victorian style drawing on medieval church architecture, featuring pointed arches, trefoils, and quatrefoils.

Pierced carving — Carved decoration cut all the way through the material, creating an openwork pattern, especially associated with laminated rosewood Rococo Revival furniture.

Quatrefoil / trefoil — Four-lobed and three-lobed decorative shapes, respectively, borrowed from Gothic architectural tracery and commonly used in Gothic Revival furniture detailing.

Renaissance Revival — A mid-Victorian style drawing on Italian and French Renaissance design, favoring symmetry, bold scale, and deep architectural carving.

Rococo Revival — A mid-Victorian style reviving 18th-century French Rococo curves, typically expressed through flowing, pierced, naturalistic carving on laminated rosewood.

Tufting (buttoning) — Upholstery technique creating a deeply padded, diamond or grid pattern through evenly spaced buttons or stitching, made widely practical by the spread of coil springs.

Materials and Finishes

This category covers the substances and treatments applied to wood and other surfaces, which affect both a piece's appearance and how it should be cleaned and cared for today.

Bloom — A cloudy, whitish haze that can develop on an old finish, often caused by moisture exposure or incompatible cleaning products.

Burl — Highly figured, swirling wood grain taken from abnormal growths on a tree, prized as a decorative veneer for its dramatic visual pattern.

Ebonized — Wood stained or finished to imitate the deep black appearance of ebony, a common decorative treatment on Renaissance Revival and Aesthetic Movement furniture.

French polish — A refined hand-applied shellac finishing technique producing a deep, glossy sheen, associated with higher-end Victorian furniture finishing.

Gilt / gilding — A thin layer of gold leaf or gold-colored material applied as decorative trim, often seen as highlights on Renaissance Revival furniture.

Marble — A natural stone widely used for washstand and dresser tops, prized for its durability and resistance to water damage from basins and pitchers used before indoor plumbing became standard.

Ormolu — Gilded bronze or brass decorative mounts, often used as embellishment on higher-end case pieces.

Papier-mâché — A molded paper-pulp material, treated and lacquered to a hard, often highly decorated finish, used for small Victorian accent pieces like trays, small tables, and decorative boxes.

Patina — The soft, mellowed sheen and color that develops on wood surfaces over decades of handling, light exposure, and natural oxidation.

Shellac — A natural resin-based finish widely used on 19th-century furniture, valued for its warm appearance but sensitive to alcohol and certain solvents.

Hardware

Small as they are, these components are frequently the fastest visual clue to a piece's style and approximate date, since hardware fashions changed in step with broader decorative trends.

Escutcheon — A protective and decorative plate surrounding a keyhole or, in some usages, a drawer pull.

Finial — A decorative knob or ornament topping a post, pediment, or other upright furniture element.

Pull (drawer pull) — The handle or knob used to open a drawer, ranging from simple turned wood to elaborate cast brass depending on style and era.

Strap hinge — A long, narrow hinge often used on chests, blanket boxes, and similar lift-top pieces, sometimes left decoratively visible rather than concealed.

Seating and Frame Terms

Apron — The horizontal structural piece running beneath a tabletop or chair seat, connecting the legs and sometimes carved or shaped decoratively.

Ball-and-claw foot — A carved foot depicting a claw gripping a ball, a decorative motif carried over from earlier furniture traditions and still seen on some Victorian revival pieces.

Cabriole leg — A curved furniture leg that flows outward at the knee and inward toward the foot, common across Rococo Revival and other Victorian styles drawing on 18th-century French design.

Crest rail — The top horizontal rail of a chair back, often the most decoratively carved element on Victorian seating.

Lyre back — A chair back shaped to resemble a classical lyre instrument, a decorative motif found on some Renaissance Revival and neoclassical-influenced Victorian chairs.

Splat — The vertical, often shaped or carved central element of a chair back, positioned between the crest rail and the seat.

Spindle — A thin, turned wooden rod used decoratively, often in rows, particularly associated with the Eastlake style's more geometric, architectural detailing.

Stretcher — A horizontal brace connecting chair or table legs lower down the frame, adding structural stability.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between an étagère and a what-not? Essentially nothing — they describe the same tiered, open display shelving form, with "étagère" being the French-derived term more often used in formal contexts and "what-not" the more colloquial English equivalent.

Why does Victorian furniture vocabulary include so many French terms? French design and cabinetmaking carried significant prestige throughout the 19th century, and many decorative and furniture-form terms (étagère, marquetry, ormolu) entered English furniture vocabulary directly from French usage during this period.

Is "Eastlake" a maker's name or a style name? It's a style name, derived from design writer Charles Eastlake's influential publications on household taste, even though Eastlake himself didn't design or manufacture most of the furniture later marketed under his name.

What does it mean if a listing says a piece has been "ebonized"? It means the wood has been stained or finished to imitate the deep black color of true ebony wood, a common and accepted decorative treatment rather than a sign of a different, less valuable underlying wood.

Why do so many of these terms relate to construction rather than appearance? Construction terminology helps collectors and appraisers communicate precisely about authenticity and dating, which often depend more on how a piece was built than on how it looks on the surface.

Final Thoughts

Fluency in Victorian furniture vocabulary pays off every time you read a listing, talk to a dealer, or try to research a piece you've found. These terms aren't just decorative jargon — each one points to a specific, often genuinely useful detail about how a piece was built, decorated, or styled, and recognizing them on sight will make you a faster, more confident reader of catalogs, auction descriptions, and your own collection alike.
